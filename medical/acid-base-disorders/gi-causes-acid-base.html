Clinical Pearl: Diarrhea vs. Laxative Abuse

These two GI conditions look similar (volume depletion, hypokalemia, low urine Cl⁻) but cause opposite acid–base disturbances. The key is what is lost in the stool and how the kidney responds.

🔴 Diarrhea (Acute) → Metabolic ACIDOSIS

The lower GI tract secretes bicarbonate-rich fluid. In acute diarrhea:

  • Massive HCO₃⁻ loss in stool
  • Serum HCO₃⁻ falls → normal anion gap metabolic acidosis
  • Kidneys cannot regenerate bicarbonate fast enough
  • Hypokalemia may occur but is not the dominant feature

🔵 Laxative Abuse (Chronic) → Metabolic ALKALOSIS

Chronic surreptitious laxative use causes a very different picture:

  • Chronic volume depletion → secondary hyperaldosteronism
  • Aldosterone drives renal H⁺ and K⁺ wasting
  • Renal H⁺ loss causes HCO₃⁻ retention → metabolic alkalosis
  • The renal effect overrides the bicarbonate lost in stool
  • Low urine Cl⁻ (<20) because of avid proximal reabsorption
Bottom line: Acute diarrhea → acidosis (bicarbonate loss). Chronic laxative abuse → alkalosis (volume depletion → secondary hyperaldosteronism → renal H⁺ and K⁺ wasting). The chronicity of the volume loss and the resulting renal compensation are what flip the acid–base status.

Check Your Understanding

1. A young man dieting for a boxing competition presents with hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, and a urine chloride of 15 mEq/L. What is the BEST next diagnostic test?

Correct: B. The combination of hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, low urine chloride (<20 mEq/L), and a history of extreme dieting strongly suggests surreptitious laxative abuse. A laxative screen confirms the diagnosis.

2. Why does ACUTE diarrhea cause metabolic acidosis, while CHRONIC laxative abuse causes metabolic alkalosis?

Correct: B. This is the key physiological paradox. In acute diarrhea, the lower GI tract loses large amounts of bicarbonate-rich fluid, causing a normal anion gap metabolic acidosis. In chronic laxative abuse, the persistent volume depletion triggers secondary hyperaldosteronism, which drives renal H⁺ and K⁺ wasting. The renal H⁺ loss causes HCO₃⁻ retention, which overrides the bicarbonate lost in the stool, resulting in metabolic alkalosis.

3. What urine chloride threshold separates chloride-responsive from chloride-resistant metabolic alkalosis?

Correct: B. A urine chloride of 20 mEq/L is the standard cutoff. Values below 20 indicate a chloride-responsive state (vomiting, NG suction, laxative abuse, remote diuretics). Values above 20 indicate chloride-resistant alkalosis (mineralocorticoid excess, Bartter/Gitelman, alkali ingestion).

4. Why does the urine chloride remain LOW in laxative abuse despite renal K⁺ wasting?

Correct: A. Volume depletion from stool losses triggers intense proximal tubular reabsorption of Na⁺ and Cl⁻, so almost no chloride reaches the urine. The urine chloride is therefore low, even though the kidney is the site of the K⁺ wasting.

5. Which of the following causes a CHLORIDE-RESISTANT metabolic alkalosis with HYPERTENSION?

Correct: C. Primary aldosteronism causes hypertension with a chloride-resistant alkalosis (urine Cl >20 mEq/L) because aldosterone drives ENaC-mediated Na⁺ retention, volume expansion, and K⁺/H⁺ wasting.

6. A patient has metabolic alkalosis with a urine chloride of 45 mEq/L and a normal blood pressure. Which diagnosis is most likely?

Correct: C. Bartter and Gitelman syndromes cause renal salt wasting with a normal or low blood pressure and a chloride-resistant alkalosis (urine Cl >20 mEq/L) due to a transporter defect and secondary hyperaldosteronism. Vomiting, NG suction, and laxative abuse all cause low urine chloride.
