Check Your Understanding

1. At what age does the diagnosis shift from FMD to Atherosclerotic RAS?

Correct: B. FMD typically presents in women aged 20–50. ARAS is a disease of older adults, with prevalence increasing after age 60. The 50–60 age range is the transitional zone where both must be considered.

2. What is the first-line treatment for ARAS in a 78-year-old with controlled hypertension and stable renal function?

Correct: B. The ASTRAL and CORAL trials showed that routine revascularization (stenting) for ARAS does not improve outcomes over aggressive medical therapy alone. Medical therapy is first-line. Revascularization is reserved for refractory hypertension, flash pulmonary edema, or rapidly worsening renal function.

3. Why is PTRA the treatment of choice for FMD but not for ARAS?

Correct: B. FMD is a localized fibroplastic lesion that dilates well with plain balloon angioplasty (PTRA). ARAS is a systemic atherosclerotic process; plain angioplasty has a high restenosis rate, so stenting is required if intervention is pursued. However, even stenting has not been shown to improve outcomes over medical therapy in most ARAS patients.

4. A 99-year-old woman is found to have a 70% ostial stenosis of the right renal artery. She has mild hypertension controlled on amlodipine. What is the most appropriate management?

Correct: C. In a 99-year-old with controlled hypertension, aggressive intervention carries a very high risk of complications (contrast nephropathy, atheroembolism, bleeding, death) with minimal expected benefit. The best approach is conservative management — continue current medical therapy and focus on quality of life.

5. What is the classic angiographic finding of FMD?

Correct: B. The "string of beads" appearance reflects alternating areas of stenosis (from fibroplasia) and post-stenotic dilatation. It is the classic angiographic hallmark of medial fibroplasia, the most common type of FMD.
