Check Your Understanding

1. A patient is found obtunded with an ethylene glycol level of 200 mg/dL, normal anion gap (10), and normal bicarbonate (24). Why is there no anion gap metabolic acidosis?

Correct: B. Ethylene glycol itself is not an acid — it is osmotically active. The high anion gap metabolic acidosis only develops after it is metabolized by alcohol dehydrogenase to glycolic acid and oxalic acid. A normal anion gap with a high ethylene glycol level means the patient is in stage 1 (early, osmolar gap phase).

2. Which two findings would you expect in a patient in stage 1 ethylene glycol toxicity?

Correct: A. In stage 1 (0–12 h), ethylene glycol is still circulating as an osmotically active parent alcohol → elevated osmolar gap. It has not yet been metabolized into acidic byproducts → normal anion gap. As metabolism proceeds (stage 2+), the osmolar gap falls and the anion gap rises.

3. Which metabolite is primarily responsible for the high anion gap metabolic acidosis in ethylene glycol toxicity?

Correct: B. Glycolic acid is the major toxic metabolite responsible for the high anion gap metabolic acidosis. Formic acid is the toxic metabolite of methanol (not ethylene glycol).

4. A 40-year-old man ingested antifreeze 30 hours ago. He now has flank pain, oliguria, and an elevated creatinine. Urinalysis shows abundant envelope-shaped crystals. What is the most likely stage of toxicity?

Correct: C. Stage 3 ethylene glycol toxicity occurs 24–72 hours after ingestion. It is characterized by calcium oxalate nephropathy with envelope-shaped calcium oxalate crystals, flank pain, hematuria, and AKI. The oxalate binds calcium, forming insoluble crystals in the renal tubules.

5. What is the treatment of choice for ethylene glycol toxicity?

Correct: C. Fomepizole (or ethanol) is the first-line antidote — it inhibits alcohol dehydrogenase, preventing formation of toxic metabolites. Hemodialysis is indicated for severe toxicity (high ethylene glycol level, severe acidosis, renal failure, or refractory symptoms). Sodium bicarbonate is supportive.

6. Why is ethanol used as an antidote for ethylene glycol poisoning?

Correct: B. Both ethanol and fomepizole act by competing for alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH). Ethanol has a higher affinity for ADH than ethylene glycol, so it blocks the conversion of ethylene glycol into its toxic metabolites (glycolic and oxalic acid). The unmetabolized ethylene glycol is then excreted unchanged by the kidneys.
