🔵 CKD Stage 3 — Mechanism of Acidosis

Reduced GFR + tubular dysfunction → impaired acid excretion

  • Stage 3 CKD: eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m² — this patient's eGFR is 44
  • Acid retention: The kidneys cannot excrete the daily acid load as ammonium (NH4+) and titratable acid[reference:0]
  • Mechanism: Reduced nephron mass → globally reduced ammonia excretion, despite increased ammoniagenesis in remaining nephrons[reference:1]
  • Result: H+ retention → metabolic acidosis with a normal anion gap (hyperchloremic)[reference:2]
  • Prevalence: Metabolic acidosis is present in ~13% of CKD 3 patients and increases with declining GFR[reference:3]

📊 Key Laboratory Interpretation

What the numbers tell us

  • pH 7.32: Acidemia (normal 7.35–7.45)
  • HCO3 19: Low (normal 22–28) — primary metabolic acidosis
  • pCO2 35: Appropriate respiratory compensation (Winter's formula: expected pCO2 = 1.5 × 19 + 8 ± 2 = 34.5–38.5)
  • Anion gap: 140 − (114 + 19) = 7 — normal anion gap (hyperchloremic acidosis)
  • Urine pH 5.0: Appropriately acidic — rules out distal RTA (which would have urine pH >5.5)
  • Urine studies: Negative protein/blood — consistent with CKD, not glomerulonephritis
🔵

Board Pearl — CKD Stage 3 Metabolic Acidosis = Impaired Acid Excretion

In CKD, metabolic acidosis develops when net acid excretion falls short of net endogenous acid production. The kidneys lose the capacity to excrete ammonium (NH4+) and titratable acid, leading to H+ retention and low serum bicarbonate[reference:4]. This is a normal anion gap (hyperchloremic) acidosis. The urine pH is appropriately acidic (5.0), distinguishing it from distal RTA (which would have urine pH >5.5).
