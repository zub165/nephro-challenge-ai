Check Your Understanding

1. A 52-year-old man on hemodialysis has recurrent predialysis sodium of 128–132 mEq/L. What is the best treatment?

Correct: C. A dialysate sodium of 135 mEq/L is slightly higher than the patient's serum sodium, creating a small gradient that gradually raises serum sodium. This is the standard approach for chronic hyponatremia in hemodialysis.

2. Why is dialysate sodium of 130 mEq/L NOT appropriate for this patient?

Correct: B. The dialysate sodium drives the serum sodium in HD. A dialysate of 130 mEq/L (equal to or lower than his serum) will not correct his hyponatremia — in fact, it may worsen it, increasing the risk of cerebral edema and seizures.

3. What is the primary risk of using a dialysate sodium of 145 mEq/L in this patient?

Correct: B. Correcting chronic hyponatremia too rapidly can cause osmotic demyelination syndrome (central pontine myelinolysis). The goal is to correct sodium slowly — usually no more than 8–10 mEq/L per 24 hours. A dialysate of 145 creates too steep a gradient and risks overly rapid correction.

4. Why are sodium chloride tablets NOT the best choice for this patient?

Correct: B. Oral sodium chloride increases serum osmolality, which triggers thirst. In a patient who is already nonadherent with fluid restriction (with >4 kg interdialytic weight gain), this will worsen volume overload, hypertension, and intradialytic complications — without fixing the underlying problem.

5. Which factor primarily determines the serum sodium concentration in a hemodialysis patient?

Correct: C. In ESRD patients on hemodialysis, serum sodium is primarily determined by the dialysate sodium concentration and the extent of ultrafiltration. The dialysate sodium sets the equilibrium point that serum sodium moves toward during each treatment. Dietary sodium and fluid intake influence weight gain but not the steady-state serum sodium set point.

6. What is the general target rate of sodium correction in chronic hyponatremia?

Correct: B. For chronic hyponatremia, the goal is to correct sodium by no more than 8–10 mEq/L in 24 hours (and 18 mEq/L in 48 hours) to avoid osmotic demyelination. The brain has adapted to the low sodium, and rapid correction can cause irreversible neurological damage.
