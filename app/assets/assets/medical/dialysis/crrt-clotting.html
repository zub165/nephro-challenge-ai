Key Features: Causes of Premature CRRT Filter Clotting

Cause Mechanism How to Detect Fix
Citrate dilution from ↑ blood flow Correct Citrate concentration in filter is proportional to citrate rate / plasma flow. When Qb ↑ from 100 → 250 mL/min, Q_p ↑ from 70 → 175 mL/min. If citrate rate unchanged, [Citrate] falls to ~40% of prior level → inadequate Ca²⁺ chelation → clot. Ionized Ca²⁺ in circuit rises (target 0.25–0.35 mmol/L); post-filter iCa rises; clot follows a Qb change without a citrate change. Increase citrate infusion rate proportionally to Qb. Monitor circuit iCa. Target 3–4 mmol/L citrate in filter.
High filtration fraction Initial Rx Postfilter replacement at 25 mL/min with Q_p 70 mL/min → FF 36%. Excessive hemoconcentration in the filter → protein/platelet concentration → clot. FF > 25%; rising TMP; dark filter; short filter life even at low Qb. Reduce postfilter replacement, move some to prefilter, or increase Qb (as the on-call did — that part was right).
Catheter dysfunction Distractor Kinking, thrombosis, or malposition → inadequate blood flow → stasis → clot. High negative access pressure; low venous return; alarms; difficulty flushing. Reposition or exchange catheter; check with fluoroscopy.
Femoral vein location Distractor Femoral catheters have slightly higher thrombosis risk but are acceptable for short-term CRRT and can support Qb up to 250 mL/min. Not a cause by itself if catheter flushes easily and pressures are acceptable. Consider IJ or subclavian if prolonged CRRT needed.
Postfilter concentrating Distractor High FF from postfilter replacement → hemoconcentration. This was the initial problem — but it was fixed by raising Qb. Already corrected by the Qb increase (FF dropped from 36% → 14%). Prefilter replacement; reduce postfilter rate.

Check Your Understanding

1. A patient on CVVHDF has an initial Qb of 100 mL/min, Hct 30%, and postfilter replacement of 1500 mL/h. What is the filtration fraction?

Correct: B. Q_p = 100 × (1 − 0.30) = 70 mL/min. Postfilter replacement = 1500 mL/h ÷ 60 = 25 mL/min. FF = 25 / 70 ≈ 36%. This is well above the target of <25% and explains the short filter life.

2. After Qb is increased to 250 mL/min, what happens to the filtration fraction?

Correct: B. Q_p = 250 × 0.70 = 175 mL/min. FF = 25 / 175 ≈ 14%. This is a healthy FF and would have reduced hemoconcentration. So the higher Qb fixed the initial high-FF problem — but introduced a new one: citrate dilution.

3. What happens to the citrate concentration in the filter when Qb is increased without changing the citrate infusion rate?

Correct: B. [Citrate] ∝ citrate rate / Q_p. When Q_p rises from 70 → 175 mL/min (a factor of 2.5), the citrate concentration falls to ~40% of its previous level. This is inadequate for anticoagulation — the filter clots.

4. In this case, what was the MOST likely cause of the filter clotting after the Qb was increased?

Correct: C. The catheter flushed easily and pressures were acceptable — so catheter issues are ruled out. FF actually decreased with higher Qb — so concentrating was not the issue. The citrate rate was unchanged while Q_p rose 2.5-fold, dropping citrate concentration in the filter to ~40% of target. This is the classic citrate-dilution clot.

5. What is the target ionized calcium in the CRRT circuit during citrate anticoagulation?

Correct: B. During citrate anticoagulation, the target ionized calcium in the circuit is 0.25–0.35 mmol/L (systemic iCa is maintained at 1.0–1.2 mmol/L with calcium replacement). If circuit iCa rises above 0.4–0.5 mmol/L, clotting is imminent.

6. How should the citrate infusion be adjusted when blood flow increases?

Correct: B. To maintain the target citrate concentration in the filter (3–4 mmol/L), the citrate infusion rate must be increased proportionally whenever Q_p rises. As a rule of thumb, if Qb doubles, the citrate rate should roughly double. Monitor circuit iCa to confirm.
