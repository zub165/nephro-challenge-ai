RAAS Cascade Activation

Renal artery stenosis reduces perfusion pressure → renin release → angiotensin II → aldosterone → hypertension, hypokalemia, and metabolic alkalosis. ACE inhibitors and ARBs block this cascade, which lowers BP but also dilates the efferent arteriole — precipitating AKI in bilateral stenosis.

Check Your Understanding

1. Which finding in this case most strongly suggests bilateral renal artery stenosis?

Correct: C. The presence of carotid and femoral bruits indicates diffuse atherosclerosis, and the refractory hypertension in an older patient strongly suggests renovascular hypertension from bilateral renal artery stenosis.

2. Why did this patient develop AKI after starting furosemide while on lisinopril?

Correct: C. In bilateral renal artery stenosis, GFR is maintained by angiotensin II–mediated efferent arteriolar constriction. ACE inhibitors (lisinopril) remove this compensation, and diuretics (furosemide) reduce volume — together, GFR collapses → AKI. This is a classic presentation of "ACE inhibitor–induced AKI" in bilateral RAS.

3. Which is the best next diagnostic test?

Correct: D. Renal artery duplex ultrasound is the non-invasive screening test of choice for renal artery stenosis. It evaluates flow velocities and anatomy without contrast or radiation, making it safe in CKD.

4. What classic electrolyte and acid–base abnormalities are expected in renovascular hypertension?

Correct: B. Renovascular hypertension activates the RAAS → aldosterone ↑ → K⁺ wasting and H⁺ secretion in the distal tubule → hypokalemia and metabolic alkalosis. The patient's K⁺ of 3.4 and HCO₃⁻ of 28 fit this picture.

5. Which of the following would best support a diagnosis of primary aldosteronism over renovascular disease?

Correct: A. Primary aldosteronism typically presents with hypertension without bruits, and the kidneys are not dependent on angiotensin II — so AKI after ACEi is not typical. The presence of diffuse bruits and AKI after ACEi strongly favors renovascular disease instead.

6. Which hemodynamic change from ACE inhibitors precipitates AKI in bilateral renal artery stenosis?

Correct: B. Angiotensin II normally constricts the efferent arteriole to maintain GFR when renal perfusion is low. ACE inhibitors block this, causing efferent dilation → GFR falls → AKI. This is the key mechanism linking bilateral RAS to ACEi-induced AKI.
